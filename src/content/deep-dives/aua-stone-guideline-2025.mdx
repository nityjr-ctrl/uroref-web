  <div class="pullquote-eyebrow">Reading the update the night before a stone MDT</div>
  <div class="pullquote-body">"I've been quoting the 2016 version confidently for years. A fair amount of it has quietly moved underneath me."</div>

Guideline updates usually feel like re-badging. A few word changes, an evidence grade nudged one way, everyone carries on. This one isn't that. Set side by side with the 2016 document, the new AUA stone guideline reads like a text written for a different operating theatre - because it was.

The clearest signal is structural. The literature search was deliberately restricted to 2000-2025 rather than reaching back to 1985, on the reasoning that pre-2000 data describe an endoscopic practice that no longer exists. GRADE was applied throughout, and the panel was multidisciplinary - endocrinology, nutrition, and patient representation alongside the endourologists. That's a different kind of document from the outset.

## The Genuinely New Statements

**Suction.** Both a suction sheath at mini-PCNL and a flexible navigable suction access sheath at URS now appear as recommendations in their own right. The accompanying editorial from Wollin, Lipkin and Preminger frames suction-enhanced and pressure-measuring systems as a sepsis-risk intervention, sitting alongside thulium fibre laser, disposable ureteroscopes and miniaturised PCNL as the technologies that have actually changed outcomes since the last edition. Intrarenal pressure has gone from a physiology-viva curiosity to something the guideline expects you to be managing.

**Thulium fibre laser given parity with holmium:YAG**, plus an explicit principle: use the lowest total power that achieves ablation. That second half matters more than the first. It's a statement about thermal injury and about the habit of turning settings up because the stone is being slow.

**Single-use versus reusable scopes** is addressed directly for the first time - Conditional, Grade A, meaning either is defensible. Useful, mostly because it stops the argument.

**Tranexamic acid at PCNL** (Conditional, A) and **PCNL without stopping low-dose aspirin** (Conditional, C). The second one is the one I'd want to be sure of before quoting it in pre-assessment, given the grade, but it's a real change in posture.

**Mini-PCNL is now a distinct modality** rather than a variant. It is preferred over URS in the 1-2 cm range, and reported as having stone-free rates equivalent to standard PCNL up to 3 cm with fewer complications and a shorter stay. If you learned stone algorithms as SWL / URS / PCNL, that's now four boxes, not three.

**Concurrent removal of asymptomatic secondary stones under 6 mm** during surgery for a symptomatic stone (Moderate, B). **Recurrent UTI with non-obstructing calyceal stones** as an indication for elective removal. Both formalise things many of us already did on instinct and then couldn't quite justify in clinic letters.

Also new: repositioning lower pole stones superiorly before lasering, multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia, secondary endoscopic clearance of residual fragments with shared decision-making, and - for SWL - slow shock rate and ramped energy as explicit technique statements.

## Strengthened

Alpha-blockers after SWL are now a **Strong** recommendation, Grade B. URS having a higher stone-free rate than SWL is now **Strong**, Grade B. Tubeless PCNL is now Grade A and explicitly independent of whether a stent is placed - which quietly separates two decisions that used to travel together in people's heads.

The odd one out is medical expulsive therapy for mid and proximal ureteric stones. It's been retained despite the panel's own meta-analysis showing no significant expulsion benefit at those sites, with a counselling caveat bolted on. I find that honest rather than evasive - they've published the negative finding and told you to say so to the patient - but it's the statement I'd expect to be challenged on.

## Narrowed

SWL keeps contracting. It's now excluded as first-line for lower pole stones over 1 cm and non-lower pole stones over 2 cm, and antibiotic prophylaxis before SWL may be omitted. The old staghorn-specific framing has been folded into the general "over 2 cm goes to PCNL" statement, which is tidier but does remove a distinct heading trainees used to revise from.

## Two Sections Worth Noticing

Pregnancy now has its own dedicated section with four statements, rather than being handled in asides. And paediatric statements are integrated wherever the evidence supports it, instead of sitting in a separate afterthought chapter.

The other detail I'd flag is in the imaging discussion: the panel explicitly acknowledges CT-based volumetric measurement, using validated segmentation or AI algorithms, as a better predictor of operative time and outcome than linear stone measurement. That is a guideline body saying, in print, that the largest-diameter number we all write in the operation note is the weaker variable.

## What I'd Take Onto a List

If I strip it back to what changes behaviour rather than what changes a slide:

- Pressure is a treatable variable. Suction and pressure-sensing aren't gadget news any more.
- Power discipline at the laser is now a stated principle, not a matter of personal style.
- 1-2 cm has a preferred answer, and it isn't URS.
- Residual fragments are a thing to plan for, not a thing to hope about.
- SWL has a narrower, better-defined lane. Know it precisely, because the lane keeps moving.

The honest caveat is that several of the new statements are Conditional, some on Grade C evidence, and the mini-PCNL comparisons draw heavily on series rather than large randomised comparisons. This is a guideline moving with the technology, which is the right instinct, but it means the confidence intervals in the room are wider than the tidy recommendation wording suggests.
