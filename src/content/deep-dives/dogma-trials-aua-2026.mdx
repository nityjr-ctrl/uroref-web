The AUA 2026 plenary in Washington DC includes a session called "When the Data Don't Fit the Dogma," led by Freddie Hamdy and featuring Seth Lerner, Brian Rini, and Ian Thompson. Four people who ran the trials that made urologists uncomfortable.

This isn't a conference summary. It's a look at the five trials I keep coming back to when I realise that something I was taught as settled fact was actually an assumption waiting to be tested.

## 1. ProtecT: Not All Prostate Cancer Needs Treating Now

Men with localised prostate cancer randomised to active monitoring, radical prostatectomy, or radiotherapy. At 15 years, prostate cancer-specific mortality was low across all three groups (2.7% overall, no significant difference). Metastases were more common with active monitoring than with radical treatment.

The old line was "definitive treatment saves lives." The honest version is more like: for many men with localised disease, survival is excellent whichever path you choose, but the trade-off is treatment toxicity versus progression risk. That's a conversation, not a prescription.

Active surveillance isn't doing nothing. It's a structured strategy to avoid overtreatment without missing the window for cure.

## 2. PIVOT: Surgery Isn't Automatically Superior

Radical prostatectomy versus observation in localised prostate cancer. Through long follow-up, surgery did not significantly reduce all-cause or prostate cancer mortality in the overall population. Some higher-risk subgroups looked like they benefited more, but the headline was clear: operating on every PSA-detected cancer doesn't automatically save lives.

The better question isn't "can we operate?" It's "is this cancer biologically significant enough, and will this patient live long enough, for radical treatment to change what actually matters?"

## 3. CARMENA: The Kidney Doesn't Always Come Out First

Metastatic RCC. For years, cytoreductive nephrectomy was reflexive: take the kidney out, then start systemic therapy. CARMENA showed sunitinib alone was non-inferior to nephrectomy-then-sunitinib in patients who needed systemic treatment.

This didn't kill cytoreductive nephrectomy. It killed the reflex that it must always come first. Patients with poor-risk disease or high metastatic burden may be harmed by delaying systemic therapy for major surgery. The point is selection, not abolition.

Worth noting: CARMENA was a targeted therapy era trial. Modern RCC management uses immunotherapy combinations, so the principle holds but the application needs current MDT interpretation.

## 4. SWOG 8710: Bladder Cancer Is Not Just a Surgical Disease

Neoadjuvant MVAC before cystectomy versus cystectomy alone for muscle-invasive bladder cancer. Median survival was 77 months with chemotherapy first versus 46 months with surgery alone.

The old thinking was "remove the bladder, job done." The trial reframed MIBC as a systemic-risk disease even when imaging looks localised. Which means the MDT question isn't just "is this patient fit for cystectomy?" but also "is this patient cisplatin-fit, and has neoadjuvant chemo been discussed?"

## 5. POUT: Upper Tract Cancer Needs More Than Nephroureterectomy

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma is awkward. Remove the kidney and ureter, and renal function drops, potentially making cisplatin-based chemotherapy impossible. POUT showed adjuvant gemcitabine-platinum chemotherapy after nephroureterectomy significantly improved disease-free survival: 71% versus 46% at three years (HR 0.45).

The old pattern was "remove and observe." The trial says high-risk UTUC needs early oncology input, and planning should start before nephroureterectomy. If adjuvant chemo is likely, post-op renal function matters, which means MDT sequencing is the whole game.

## The Actual Lesson

None of these trials gave us a simple new rule. They gave us better frameworks and harder conversations.

The immature response to any of them is to swap one dogma for another: "never operate," "always surveil," "nobody needs cytoreductive nephrectomy." That's not evidence-based medicine. That's just new dogma wearing a statistical coat.

The mature response is messier. Which patients were included? What was the control arm? What era of systemic therapy? Was the endpoint survival, metastasis, recurrence, or quality of life? Does this apply to the person sitting in front of me?

A good trial doesn't make the clinic simpler. It makes the consent conversation more honest.
