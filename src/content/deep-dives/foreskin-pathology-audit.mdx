End of a straightforward circumcision for phimosis, and the pot goes to pathology. "Just in case." I've written that more times than I can count, and I couldn't tell you what we actually find.

A group in Edinburgh looked at 2,503 adult circumcision specimens sent to pathology at the Western General between 2015 and early 2025. Sixty-six came back as cancer, 2.6%. Most already had a biopsy, or a growth or red patch written in the notes. That left 14 cancers in men listed as simple phimosis, with nothing worrying written down. So it took **109** routine pots to find one unexpected cancer.

Only one of those fourteen needed anything beyond the circumcision already done, a topical treatment to the glans. To catch that one, they had to send 1,532 pots with no documented suspicion. The pathology bill for all 2,503 over the period was £65,584. That works out at £994 for each cancer of any sort, and £65,600 for the one that actually changed what anyone did.

Now the catch. It's one centre and retrospective, and they started from pots that arrived in the lab, so this is not a complete list of every circumcision. "Suspicion" was any red patch or growth in the notes, which is a wide net, and "cancer" includes carcinoma in situ. They don't say how many of the fourteen were invasive. The costs cover processing and reporting only. The Royal College of Pathologists had already listed routine circumcision specimens as of limited or no clinical value. This is the local arithmetic.

I'll still send the ones that look wrong.

I'd love to claim I've been selective for years. My last op note still said "histology, please".
