When I wrote about Roen Surgical's Zamenix in June, the section I was most confident about was the list of things we didn't know. Near the top of it: the device had cleared a Korean innovative medical technology assessment, and CE marking and FDA clearance were entirely separate processes with no timeline anyone would commit to.

Roen Surgical is now reported to have won FDA clearance - described as the first for an AI-based kidney stone surgery robot. That moves robotic RIRS out of the "interesting thing happening in Korea" category and into a regulatory pathway that ends, eventually, in a procurement conversation somewhere near me.

Which is exciting, and which is also the moment to be careful about what a clearance actually means. Because I think a lot of people are going to read the headline as evidence, and it isn't evidence. It's permission.

## What the Device Is, Briefly

Covered this in June, so the short version. Zamenix is a robotic system built specifically for retrograde intrarenal surgery rather than adapted from a general surgical platform: a 2.8 mm flexible endoscope driven by a motorised robot, controlled from a master console, with the scope held in position between adjustments instead of by the surgeon's hands. The features the company leads on are AI assistance and respiratory motion compensation - accounting for the kidney moving with the breathing cycle while the laser is firing.

The problem it targets is real. Standard fURS means holding a heavy handle through 30 to 60 minutes of small repetitive movements, often in lead. Ergonomic injury in high-volume stone surgeons is documented, not theoretical.

## The Clinical Data Behind It

The confirmatory Korean trial enrolled 46 patients with stones from 0.5 to 3 cm undergoing RIRS. Reported stone removal rate 93.5%, mild complication rate 6.5%. In April this year the company reported completing a domestic clinical trial for robot-assisted flexible ureteroscopic stone removal - reported as the first completed invasive innovative medical technology trial of its kind in Korea. Deployments have accumulated at Samsung Medical Center, Korea University Anam, Seoul National University Bundang, a training centre at SNU, and an export installation in Indonesia.

Ninety-three point five percent sounds excellent. Sit with it for a second, though.

Forty-six patients is small. There is no randomised comparator arm, so the number floats free of what an experienced surgeon would have achieved with a standard scope on the same stones - and in good hands, stone-free rates in that size range are already high. The stone size band runs from 0.5 cm, which is not where anyone struggles, up to 3 cm, which very much is, and a single pooled figure hides how it performed at each end. And "stone removal rate" is only interpretable if you know how it was assessed. The new AUA guideline is pushing towards thin-slice CT and volumetric assessment precisely because the older ways of counting stone-free flatter everyone.

None of this makes the device bad. It makes the number preliminary.

## Clearance Is Not the Same as Superiority

This is the bit I'd want to have straight before saying anything confident in a departmental meeting, because I nearly got it wrong myself.

FDA clearance is a regulatory judgement about whether a device is safe and performs as intended - and, in the most common route, whether it is substantially equivalent to something already on the market. It is not a finding that the device produces better outcomes than the alternative. A cleared device can be equivalent, and clearance is entirely compatible with "works about as well, through a different mechanism."

So the honest reading of this news is: a regulator has looked at the safety and performance package and let it onto the US market. That is a genuine milestone, and it is the necessary first step for any of the rest. It is not a stone-free rate.

I'd also want to read the actual clearance wording rather than the coverage - what specific indication it covers, which pathway it went through, and what limitations sit in the labelling. Headlines compress all of that into three words, and the three words are always the flattering ones.

## What It Changes for Us

Not much yet, and that's fine.

A US clearance doesn't confer UKCA or CE status, and UK availability runs through its own route. Beyond regulatory approval there's the part nobody puts in a press release: capital cost, disposables per case, theatre footprint, the training programme, and a cost-effectiveness case in a system where the comparator is a consultant with a scope who is already very good at this.

What it does change is the seriousness of the question. Robotic RIRS is no longer something to file under "watch this space." Somebody will present a UK business case for something in this category within the next few years, and the useful thing a registrar can do now is know what to ask when it happens.

## What I'd Want Before Believing the Story

- A randomised comparison against standard fURS, with stone-free rate on thin-slice CT as the primary outcome.
- Outcomes broken out by stone size and location - particularly lower pole, and particularly above 2 cm, where the new AUA guidance already prefers mini-PCNL.
- Complication and reintervention rates, not just the index procedure.
- Radiation and operative time, including the setup and docking that never appear in the "procedure time" figure.
- Honest learning curve data. Yeungnam reported times falling with experience, which is encouraging and also means early results are not steady-state results.
- Whether the ergonomic benefit is measured rather than asserted. If the case for the robot is surgeon musculoskeletal health, someone should measure surgeon musculoskeletal health.

## The Bit I Can't Argue Away

I've spent this whole piece hedging, and I stand by the hedges. But there's a version of this technology's story where I look back at my June caution and find it slightly silly, because the direction of travel is not subtle. Console-driven, motion-compensated, remotely operable endoscopy solves problems that are genuinely ours: ergonomic injury, tremor, the physical grind of a long stone list, and eventually the geography of who can get a scope to a stone at all.

The evidence isn't there yet. The regulatory door just opened anyway. Both things are true, and the gap between them is where the next two years of stone surgery arguments are going to happen.
